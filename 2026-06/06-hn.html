The Silent Revolution: Why the World Is Running Out of Children

The global fertility rate has halved since 1965, dropping from 5 children per woman to below 2.5, as women gain education and labor market access, child mortality falls, and the costs of raising children rise; this decline is reshaping economies and societies worldwide, with Our World in Data documenting the rapid shift and The Economist warning of India’s surprise baby bust as a global omen.

For most of human history, families routinely had five or more children, but the last two centuries marked a turning point. Improved healthcare and education empowered women to choose smaller families. Declining child mortality reduced the perceived need for large families as insurance against early deaths. Urbanization and the end of child labor increased the financial burden of raising children, making large families less practical. The result is a world where 80% of people now live in countries with fertility rates below 3 children per woman, and population growth has slowed from 2.3% annually in 1963 to under 1% today. Countries like Iran and Thailand have seen fertility rates plummet from over 6 children per woman in the 1950s to near or below replacement level today. This demographic transition marks the end of rapid global population growth, replacing high mortality with low fertility as the primary check on population expansion.

The implications are vast. Economies built on endless labor supply growth face stagnation as workforces shrink. Social systems from pensions to healthcare, designed for expanding populations, may collapse under the weight of aging citizens. Immigration becomes both more critical and more contentious as countries scramble to fill gaps. The shift also reflects broader societal changes—women’s empowerment, urbanization, and the rising cost of living—which are unlikely to reverse. While some nations may see temporary rebounds, the long-term trend points toward a smaller, older global population, forcing a reevaluation of economic models, social contracts, and geopolitical power structures.
