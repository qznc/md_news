Elevator Inefficiencies: Why Algorithms Matter for Your Daily Commute

The frustration of waiting endlessly for an elevator is a shared human experience, yet the systems behind these delays are remarkably complex and often riddled with hidden inefficiencies. This article explores how outdated or poorly optimized elevator algorithms can lead to unnecessary wait times, drawing on insights from real-world discussions and technical analyses. For something as mundane as vertical transport, the interplay of logic and engineering reveals fascinating opportunities for improvement.

The Legacy of Simple Algorithms Elevator systems have long relied on basic algorithms like SCAN and LOOK, which were developed decades ago. The SCAN algorithm moves elevators linearly from basement to top floor and back, while LOOK improves this by reversing direction only when needed. However, these methods prioritize simplicity over adaptability. In the john.fun/elevators article, the LOOK algorithm’s rigid structure becomes a liability during peak traffic, such as morning rushes in office buildings, where wait times spike due to inefficiencies in handling concentrated demands.

The Rise of Smarter Systems: RSR and Beyond Modern systems like Otis’ RSR algorithm introduce nuanced optimizations. RSR dynamically scores elevators based on estimated time of arrival, passenger load, and directional alignment. For instance, it penalizes elevators already heading to a crowded floor or rewards those idle near a call. This adaptability reduces wait times by ~15% compared to LOOK in high-traffic scenarios, as highlighted in the same source. However, RSR’s success isn’t universal. In smaller buildings or low-flow situations, simpler algorithms may perform better, illustrating the need for context-aware solutions.

Destination Dispatch: More Information, Less Flexibility? A counterintuitive twist emerges with Destination Dispatch (DD), where kiosks direct users to elevators based on their exact destination. While DD provides precise data, it often backfires. The john.fun/elevators analysis found DD could worsen waits because it locks passengers into specific elevators, preventing real-time re-optimization. HN commenter omoikane noted that in practice, DD’s rigid routing fails to adapt to dynamic conditions, like sudden crowd surges or elevator delays, making it less effective than manual button presses in most cases.
