# Silent Browser Fingerprinting and Its Hardware Consequences  

[Browser fingerprinting](https://blog.laserphile.com/2026/08/aliexpress-webpage-keeping-multipoint.html) refers to techniques websites use to identify devices or users without relying on cookies. A recent discovery involving AliExpress revealed that silent [WebAudio fingerprinting](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Web_Audio_API) can interfere with hardware functionality—specifically, [Bluetooth multipoint](https://www.bluetooth.com/blog/bluetooth-multipoint-explained/) connections. This intersection of privacy invasion and unintended hardware disruption highlights growing concerns over opaque tracking practices.

## WebAudio Fingerprinting Mechanism  

Websites exploit the [WebAudio API](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Web_Audio_API) to create low-level audio processing graphs. These graphs often include oscillators, analyzers, and gain nodes. While these nodes simulate audio signals, they remain silent due to gain set to zero. However, the browser still processes these signals internally, which can uniquely fingerprint a device based on its hardware and software configuration. AliExpress employed this method via obfuscated scripts (`[collina.js](https://assets.aliexpress-media.com/g/AWSC/uab/1.140.0/collina.js)` and `[fireyejs.js](https://assets.aliexpress-media.com/g/AWSC/fireyejs/1.231.67/fireyejs.js)`), generating a comprehensive fingerprint including canvas rendering, WebGL details, and device characteristics.

## Hardware Disruption Side Effect  

For users with [multipoint Bluetooth headphones](https://www.soundguys.com/bluetooth-multipoint-explained-8391018/), AliExpress’s silent audio processing caused their devices to lock onto the PC audio stream. Even after closing tabs or muting them, the Bluetooth connection remained active, disrupting phone calls and music streaming. Unlike traditional media elements, tab muting had no effect because the [AudioContext](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/AudioContext) wasn’t tied to audible output. This behavior demonstrates how deeply embedded browser APIs can unintentionally conflict with hardware logic.

## Broader Privacy Implications  

Fingerprinting scripts like those found on AliExpress gather vast amounts of data silently, combining audio, visual, and behavioral traces. As noted in [Hacker News discussions](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49372583), such methods are not isolated incidents. Sites like Twitter and platforms using [WebRTC](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/WebRTC_API) for fingerprinting further blur the line between functionality and surveillance. Users lack visibility into these processes, raising ethical questions about consent and transparency.

## Mitigation Strategies  

Blocking specific script families via ad blockers like [uBlock Origin](https://ublockorigin.com/) offers temporary relief. Filters targeting AliExpress’s fingerprinting scripts prevent AudioContexts from initializing, restoring normal Bluetooth behavior. However, this approach is fragile—obfuscation and evolving techniques mean new scripts could emerge. Some propose permission-gating audio APIs or sandboxing build environments, akin to proposals for securing [Rust crate ecosystems](https://safedep.io/arrayref-proc-macro1-rust-build-time-malware/). Until systemic changes occur, proactive blocking and informed browsing habits remain critical defenses against silent tracking.
