# The Meat Proxy Problem: Why AI Output Without Human Filtering Is Killing Technical Communication

AI has become the new middleman in technical workflows, but the delegation of actual thinking to machines is creating a new class of inefficiency—engineers acting as "meat proxies" who simply relay AI-generated responses without adding value.

[In a recent blog post](https://gruhn.me/blog/2026-08-03/), an engineer described the growing frustration with colleagues who respond to questions with verbatim AI output. Whether it’s Slack threads, pull request feedback, or casual conversations, the pattern repeats: someone asks a question, gets a wall of AI-generated text in response, and is left to parse jargon-heavy content that often makes little sense in context. This isn’t helpful—it’s noise.

The core issue isn’t that people are using AI tools like Claude or Gemini. It’s that they’re treating these tools as replacements for their own reasoning. For example, during code reviews, some developers copy-paste ticket descriptions into AI coding assistants like Claude Code, generate code without reviewing it, and then ask others to validate the output. When problems arise, they forward AI-generated explanations without understanding them themselves.

On [Hacker News, users echoed these frustrations](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49151933), sharing anecdotes of being asked to “read this 300-line response from Claude and tell me if it’s right.” One commenter noted that even junior and senior engineers fall into this trap, forwarding AI summaries without context or comprehension. Another recalled a peer who responded with, “thanks but I can ask Claude myself,” effectively calling out the useless intermediary.

Some defend the practice, citing cases where AI output is accurate enough to share directly. One user explained that their team uses AI responsibly—interpreting results before passing them along—and finds it accelerates decision-making. However, this approach relies heavily on trust, shared understanding, and the competence of the person acting as proxy.

The problem intensifies when proxies don’t identify themselves. One commenter shared experiences with individuals forwarding AI responses without attribution, leaving recipients unsure whether they’re engaging with a human or machine. This ambiguity erodes accountability and muddies communication channels.

Language also plays a role. AI-generated text tends to be dense and filled with technical jargon, making it harder to digest. Some suggest prompting models to simplify language or use formats like ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English to improve clarity. Others advocate for switching to native languages or adjusting settings to reduce verbosity.

Beyond workplace dynamics, there are deeper concerns about skill erosion. As reliance on AI grows, so does the risk of losing critical thinking abilities. One developer joked about becoming “the condom between Claude Code and prod,” highlighting fears that engineers are becoming mere facilitators rather than creators.

Companies are starting to take notice. Some have implemented AI codes of conduct or begun terminating employees who contribute nothing beyond relaying AI output. In extreme cases, teams have discovered that entire codebases were generated without human oversight, leading to catastrophic rework and wasted resources.

The broader lesson is clear: AI should augment human intelligence, not replace it. A thoughtful response—even a brief summary of AI insights—demonstrates engagement and adds value. Simply forwarding raw AI output strips away nuance, reduces trust, and wastes everyone’s time.

As [one SecurityWeek article illustrates](https://research.jfrog.com/post/sqlite-critical-cves-or-llm-slops/), the consequences of unchecked AI automation extend beyond internal teams. Fabricated vulnerability advisories generated by AI were mistakenly flagged as critical by databases and scanners, triggering unnecessary panic and remediation efforts. These "LLM slop" CVEs highlight how uncritical adoption of AI tools can lead to systemic errors with real-world impact.

Ultimately, the goal isn’t to reject AI but to integrate it thoughtfully. Teams must balance efficiency gains with personal responsibility, ensuring that AI serves as a tool—not a crutch. The future belongs to those who can harness AI while maintaining ownership of their decisions and communications.
