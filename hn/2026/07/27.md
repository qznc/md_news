# Navigating Duress: Encryption, Borders, and the GrapheneOS Wipe

A US citizen’s arrest after triggering a privacy-protective wipe on his GrapheneOS phone at an airport shines a harsh light on the collision between strong cryptography and border-search powers. The case, highlighted on Hacker News, raises uncomfortable questions about whether defending your data can itself become a crime [1](https://www.techspot.com/news/113236-us-prosecutors-charge-atlanta-man-after-grapheneos-phone.html).

GrapheneOS is a security-focused, open-source operating system for Google Pixel phones that offers a feature called a “Duress PIN/Password.” When entered at the lock screen or any other credential prompt, it irreversibly wipes the device and any installed eSIMs, with no reboot required and no way to interrupt it [2](https://grapheneos.org/features#duress). The feature is designed for scenarios involving robbery or state coercion, letting a user protect their data by making it impossible to access. The recent federal case against Sam Tunick alleges he used this feature at Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport to destroy evidence after agents repeatedly demanded he unlock his phone [1](https://www.techspot.com/news/113236-us-prosecutors-charge-atlanta-man-after-grapheneos-phone.html).

Prosecutors are pursuing Tunick under a federal statute that criminalizes destroying property to prevent its seizure, even though the incident occurred during a border search. Commentators on Hacker News quickly noted a legal gap in the indictment; the statute targets sabotage to avoid seizure, not the act of refusing a search by wiping a device. Some legal observers speculate the case may be dismissed because the agents may not have had grounds to seize the phone in the first place [3](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49063022).

The discussion reveals a deep divide between technical and legal approaches to the threat model at the US border. EFF staffer schoen, who co-authored a border-search guide years ago, wrote that the power imbalance at the border is enormous and that technical “non-compliance” can be naive if it antagonizes agents without a broader strategy [3](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49063022). Another commenter noted that while you cannot make wiping a phone illegal—if it’s just keystrokes—the legal system will judge what you were trying to do, not just what you did [4](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49063022). This reflects the legal view that a “duress PIN” intended to foil robbers is different from one used against state agents.

The practical risk is the harshest takeaway. Commenting from a European perspective, one user noted that the standard professional advice for high-risk travel is to back up the phone, wipe it, and restore it on the other side of the border. In the US, the same tactic now carries a risk of prosecution [5](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49063022). A more nuanced technical response from another Hacker News reader suggests a layered approach: travel with a minimal “burner” phone set up with a clean, encrypted backup profile and no personal accounts. If the agent demands the PIN, the user can cooperate with a device that contains no real data, avoiding both legal jeopardy and the permanent loss of a primary device [6](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49063022).

The GrapheneOS team’s documentation explicitly states that the duress feature performs a full, irreversible wipe. It is not a hidden “sneaky” backdoor but a deliberately conspicuous action that requires both a PIN and a password to trigger, a design choice to prevent accidental activation [2](https://grapheneos.org/features#duress). The case underscores that this feature, when deployed at a border, transforms from a last-resort privacy tool into an act with immediate, severe legal consequences. Whether Tunick’s prosecution holds up, the precedent is chilling: for now, a US citizen’s choice to use strong encryption at the border can be framed as obstruction or destruction of evidence [1](https://www.techspot.com/news/113236-us-prosecutors-charge-atlanta-man-after-grapheneos-phone.html).
