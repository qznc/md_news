# Open‑Weight LLMs ≠ Open Research: The Missing Piece Is Transparent Training Frameworks  

Open weights let anyone download a large language model, but they don’t reveal **how** the model was trained or how to modify that process.  
Without an open, extensible training stack, reproducing state‑of‑the‑art results or inventing new algorithms remains a black‑box exercise.  

Researchers need code that exposes every step—from data loading to roll‑out generation, reward calculation, loss construction, and weight syncing—so that algorithms stay **algorithms** and systems stay **systems**.  
Frameworks like **FeynRL** and **OpenRLHF** provide exactly that level of visibility, while projects such as **LLaMA‑Factory** show how a unified, open‑source stack can scale from a single GPU to multi‑node clusters.  

The Reddit discussion on open weights stresses that “open weights are important and critical, but they are not enough by themselves” — the community is calling for open training pipelines that are *understandable* and *modifiable* [Open‑weight debate on Reddit](https://old.reddit.com/r/MachineLearning/comments/1u6p7k3/open_weights_are_not_enough_we_need_open_training/) .  
Without such frameworks, anyone trying to replicate a near‑SOTA model must wrestle with hidden rollout engines, credit‑assignment bugs, and opaque distributed synchronization, which slows progress and entrenches closed‑source advantages [Comment by dalhaze](https://old.reddit.com/r/MachineLearning/comments/1u6p7k3/open_weights_are_not_enough_we_need_open_training/) .  

**FeynRL** was created for exactly this purpose.  
Its “algorithm‑first” design keeps the training loop explicit: data feeds, roll‑out workers, reward modules, and optimization steps are each a separate, readable component [FeynRL repository](https://github.com/FeynRL-project/FeynRL) .  
The framework supports supervised fine‑tuning (SFT), preference learning (DPO), and a full suite of reinforcement‑learning methods (PPO, GRPO, CISPO, P3O) across single‑GPU, multi‑GPU, and large‑scale cluster setups.  
Because the algorithmic logic lives locally while the system layer stays explicit, developers can add a new loss term or reward function without rewriting the orchestration code.  

**OpenRLHF** takes a similar philosophy but focuses on reinforcement learning from human feedback.  
Built on a Ray + vLLM distributed backbone, it introduces a unified **agent‑based execution** model that cleanly separates token‑in‑token‑out generation from the RL algorithm [OpenRLHF repository](https://github.com/OpenRLHF/OpenRLHF) .  
This architecture enables both single‑turn and multi‑turn training, supports asynchronous pipelines for higher throughput, and works with a wide range of RL algorithms (PPO, REINFORCE++, GRPO, RLOO).  
By exposing the full training pipeline, OpenRLHF makes it feasible to experiment with custom reward functions, multi‑modal agents, and large‑scale deployments without hidden “magic”.  

**LLaMA‑Factory** demonstrates that an open, feature‑rich stack can also serve a broader audience.  
It bundles supervised fine‑tuning, reward modeling, and RLHF (including DPO and PPO) behind a zero‑code CLI and a web UI, while still leveraging the same underlying distributed infrastructure (DeepSpeed, Ray, vLLM).  
Because it supports LoRA, QLoRA, and many optimizer tricks, LLaMA‑Factory shows that extensibility does not have to come at the cost of performance [LLaMA‑Factory repository](https://github.com/hiyouga/LLaMA-Factory) .  

Together, these projects illustrate a clear pattern: **open weights become truly open only when paired with open training frameworks that make the entire learning loop inspectable and mutable**.  
Adopting such frameworks lowers the barrier for reproducibility, accelerates algorithmic innovation, and prevents the closed‑source advantage from widening.
